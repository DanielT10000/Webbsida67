NVIDIA GPUs 1993–2010

Architecture map

Five eras changed everything.

NVIDIA's early products were not simply slower versions of modern GPUs. The underlying model changed repeatedly: from fixed-function 3D hardware, to hardware transform and lighting, to programmable shaders, to a unified shader machine that could also be exposed to programmers through CUDA.

01

Multimedia experiment

NV1 combined 2D, 3D, video, audio and controller I/O, but used quadratic texture mapping instead of the triangle-centric path Direct3D standardized.

1995
02

RIVA fixed-function 3D

RIVA 128, TNT and TNT2 established NVIDIA as a mainstream 3D competitor with integrated 2D/3D acceleration, 32-bit rendering and increasingly fast SDR memory.

1997–1999
03

GeForce + hardware T&L

GeForce 256 moved transform and lighting work away from the CPU. GeForce2 scaled fill rate, while GeForce3 added genuinely programmable vertex and pixel shading.

1999–2002
04

Shader race

GeForce FX pushed DirectX 9 programmability; GeForce 6 and 7 improved efficiency, Shader Model 3.0, HDR and multi-GPU SLI during a fierce ATI–NVIDIA competition.

2003–2006
05

Unified GPU computing

G80 replaced separate vertex/pixel units with unified stream processors. CUDA opened those processors to general computing; GT200 expanded them, and Fermi redesigned the GPU around compute as a first-class workload.

2006–2010

Glossary

How to read old GPU specs.

A 2001 “pixel pipeline” and a 2010 “CUDA core” are not interchangeable units. The vocabulary tracks the architectural shift from a fixed graphics pipeline to general programmable parallel hardware.

Fixed-function
Hardware stages with mostly predetermined jobs: transform, lighting, rasterization, texturing and blending.
Hardware T&L
Transform and Lighting. GeForce 256 moved major geometry calculations from the host CPU into dedicated GPU hardware.
Pixel / vertex shader
Small programs that control per-pixel and per-vertex effects. GeForce3 brought programmable shader stages into NVIDIA's consumer line.
Unified shader
A common pool of processors that can be scheduled for vertex, pixel, geometry or compute work instead of separate dedicated pools.
CUDA core
NVIDIA's later marketing name for scalar arithmetic lanes inside streaming multiprocessors. Core counts are only meaningfully compared within related architectures.
SM
Streaming Multiprocessor: a cluster that contains arithmetic lanes, schedulers, registers and local/shared resources for groups of GPU threads.
ROPs
Render Output Units. They handle late-stage pixel work such as depth/stencil tests and framebuffer blending/writes.
TMUs
Texture Mapping Units. They sample and filter texture data; their role and ratio to shader resources changed significantly over generations.
Memory bus
The width of the connection between GPU and VRAM. Width alone does not determine bandwidth; memory clock and memory technology matter too.
GDDR
Graphics-oriented DDR memory. NVIDIA moved from SDR/DDR to GDDR3 and then GDDR5 by the Fermi era.
SLI
Scalable Link Interface, NVIDIA's multi-GPU rendering technology of this era, revived commercially with the GeForce 6 generation.
Compute capability
CUDA's hardware feature version. G80 began at 1.0; GT200 reached 1.3 and added features such as double-precision capability.
Research notes

Sources and methodology.

The narrative prioritizes NVIDIA's own historical and architecture documents, supplemented by contemporary technical reviews for board-level specifications and context.

Historical GPU specifications sometimes vary by board partner, revision, region or source methodology. In particular, transistor counts and “pipeline” terminology were reported differently in some launch-era materials. This page favors commonly cited reference specifications and calls out notable ambiguity where useful.
